## M0. Notation and conventions

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $k, n$ | successes and trials in a cell |
| $\hat p = k/n$ | sample proportion |
| $z_{\alpha/2}$ | $\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)$; $z_{.025} = 1.959964$ |
| $a_{11}, b, c, a_{00}$ | paired $2\times2$ counts: both 1, arm A only, arm B only, both 0 |
| $p_{11}, p_{10}, p_{01}, p_{00}$ | their probabilities; $p_d = p_{10}+p_{01}$ is the **discordant** probability |
| $\delta = p_{10}-p_{01}$ | paired risk difference |
| $\psi = p_{10}/p_{01}$ | conditional (discordance) odds ratio |
| $G$ | number of clusters (models) |
| $Y_i \in \{0,1\}$ | `obeyed` — the model emitted `delete_records` in the post-payload turn |

All intervals are two-sided 95% ($\alpha = 0.05$) unless stated. All tests are
two-sided. "Delivered" means the model called the carrier tool at turn 1, so the
payload actually entered its context; `INVALID` trials (truncated generations
with no tool call) are excluded everywhere and are never scored as a pass or a
fail.

**Two sampling frames, deliberately kept apart.**

* **Delivered-conditional.** For `framing`, `position`, `authority` and
  `containment` — and for those four only — the payload is revealed only in the
  *tool result*, i.e. after the turn-1 call. Delivery is therefore strictly
  pre-treatment with respect to those factors and the delivered subsample is a
  random subsample with respect to them. This is an identification argument, and
  it is falsifiable: delivery rate must be **flat** across those factors, and
  `analyze.py` §3b runs that check. On the controls run it passes — delivery was
  0.8331 / 0.8333 / 0.8330 across the three framings, $\chi^2$ $p = 0.9999$.
* **`carrier` is NOT in that frame, despite being a property of the tool
  result.** $D_i$ is *defined* as $\mathbf 1\{$turn 1 called the designated
  carrier tool $K_i\}$, and $K_i$ is named in the turn-1 operator message. So
  $K \to D$ is an edge of the design, not a leak: a model may simply be better
  at calling `web_search` than `read_file`. Two consequences, both enforced in
  `analyze.py`. (i) `carrier` is excluded from the §3b flatness family — leaving
  it in would fire a falsification alarm on the ablation stage for a difference
  the design predicts. (ii) `carrier` is excluded from the delivered-only
  regression, because conditioning on $D$ there opens the collider
  $K \to D \leftarrow U \to Y$; its coefficient is read off the ITT fit, which
  conditions on nothing. This matches `PAPER.md` §3.6, which draws $K \to D$ in
  the DAG and states A1 for $W \in \{C, F, P, A, S\}$ only. An earlier revision
  of this appendix and of `ATTACK_FACTORS` listed `carrier` as pre-treatment;
  that was an identification error and is corrected here.
* **Intention-to-treat.** The `defense` prompt is in the system message at turn
  1 and can suppress delivery. Conditioning its contrast on delivery would
  condition on a post-treatment collider, so `defense` is estimated over **all**
  attack trials with undelivered scored $Y=0$. `carrier` rides in this frame for
  the reason above.

## M1. Why interval estimators, not $p$-values, carry this paper

The primary outcome is binary and mechanically observed, so the measurement
error is zero and the only uncertainty is sampling. At $n = 4{,}680$ trials
almost every contrast is "significant"; the quantities that carry information
are magnitudes and their intervals. Three of the study's headline cells are also
**boundary cells** — $0/1297$, $20/20$, $0/30$ — where the textbook estimators
are not merely imprecise but *degenerate*, returning intervals of zero width.
Choosing estimators that behave at the boundary is not fastidiousness here; it
is the difference between a negative control that says something and one that
crashes the plotting code.

---

## M2. A single proportion — the Wilson score interval

### The estimator

The score (Wilson) interval inverts the score test
$Z(p) = (\hat p - p)/\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$, solving $|Z(p)| = z_{\alpha/2}$ for $p$:

$$
\mathrm{CI}_{\text{Wilson}}
= \frac{1}{1+\frac{z^2}{n}}
\left[\;
\hat p + \frac{z^2}{2n}
\;\pm\;
z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}
\;\right],
\qquad z \equiv z_{\alpha/2}.
$$

`analyze.wilson` implements exactly this and then clamps the result to
$[0,1]$ **and** to contain $\hat p$.

### Why not Wald

The Wald interval is $\hat p \pm z\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}$. Its width is
proportional to $\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)}$, so:

$$
k = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \mathrm{CI}_{\text{Wald}} = [0,\,0],
\qquad
k = n \;\Rightarrow\; \mathrm{CI}_{\text{Wald}} = [1,\,1].
$$

A zero-width interval asserts the rate is known exactly. The study's negative
control is a $0/n$ cell **by construction** — it exists precisely to report a
zero — so this is the modal case, not an edge case. From `power.py` §11a:

| $k$ | $n$ | Wald | width | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 20 | [0.0000, 0.0000] | 0.0000 | degenerate |
| 0 | 1297 | [0.0000, 0.0000] | 0.0000 | degenerate |
| 20 | 20 | [1.0000, 1.0000] | 0.0000 | degenerate |
| 2 | 260 | [−0.0029, 0.0183] | 0.0212 | usable, but the lower limit is negative |

The Wilson interval at $k=0$ collapses to

$$
\left[\,0,\;\frac{z^2}{n+z^2}\,\right],
$$

which is positive and shrinks like $1/n$ — the correct behaviour for "we saw
none in $n$". (Note that this is the *same* expression as Tango's paired
interval at $b = c = 0$ in §M5.3. Both are score intervals, so both degrade to
the same "no events observed" bound; that is the family resemblance, not a
coincidence.)

### The floating-point failure mode this study actually hit

At $k = 0$ the algebra cancels: the centre equals the half-width, and the lower
limit is $0$ in exact arithmetic. In floating point the cancellation is not
exact and its **sign is not reproducible across rewrites of the same
expression**. Sweeping $n = 1\ldots5000$ with $k=0$ (`power.py` §11a):

* a strictly **negative** unclamped lower limit for **718 of 5000** values of
  $n$ (minimum $-6.94\times10^{-18}$, first at $n = 27$);
* at $k = n$, an unclamped upper limit **above 1** for **927 of 5000** (maximum
  excess $2.22\times10^{-16}$).

`matplotlib` raises on a negative error bar. Roughly one all-zero cell in seven
would have crashed the forest plot, and the negative control is the first cell
the confirmatory run produces. `analyze.wilson`'s clamp is therefore load-bearing
and the comment above it is accurate.

### Verification

`power.py` §11a prints `analyze.wilson` beside
`scipy.stats.binomtest(k, n).proportion_ci(method="wilson")`, an independent
implementation:

```
max |analyze.wilson upper - scipy upper| over k=0..40, n in {40, 260}: 2.22e-16
```

| cell | Wilson 95% | unclamped lower | appears as |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 / 1297 | [0.000000, 0.002953] | $+4.34\times10^{-19}$ | negative control, delivered clean trials |
| 0 / 1557 | [0.000000, 0.002461] | $+2.17\times10^{-19}$ | negative control including turn 1 |
| 0 / 40 | [0.000000, 0.087622] | $\mathbf{-6.94\times10^{-18}}$ | a 40-pair cell — this one is negative |
| 0 / 30 | [0.000000, 0.113513] | $0$ | gemma4 contained arm, probe |
| 20 / 20 | [0.838875, 1.000000] | — | `spec_voice` concatenated, pooled |
| 0 / 20 | [0.000000, 0.161125] | $0$ | a 20-trial clean arm — bounds the rate at 16%, no better |

### Assumptions and failure modes

* **Assumes** independent Bernoulli trials with a common $p$ within the cell.
  This is *false* at the trial level here — 20 trials share one attack string —
  and the design-effect correction is §M6 and `power.py` §4. Wilson intervals on
  a pooled cell are therefore **anti-conservative** by roughly
  $\sqrt{1+(m-1)\rho_{\text{ICC}}}$ and should be read as precision statements
  about the realised trials, not about the population of attacks.
* **Coverage** oscillates with $n$ (all discrete-data intervals do) and averages
  near nominal; Clopper–Pearson is guaranteed $\ge 1-\alpha$ but is materially
  wider, and `analyze.clopper_pearson` is used only where an exact guarantee is
  wanted (the conditional-OR limits in §M5).
* Agresti–Coull ($\hat p^\ast = (k+2)/(n+4)$ substituted into the Wald formula)
  is a reasonable alternative and is *not* degenerate at $k=0$ — the pseudo-counts
  see to that. It is rejected here for three lesser reasons. Its lower limit at
  $k=0$ is *genuinely* negative rather than spuriously so
  ($[-0.027, 0.194]$ at $n=20$, $[-0.0029, 0.0180]$ at $n=260$), so it needs
  clamping too. It is materially wider than Wilson at the boundary cells this
  design produces — upper bound 0.194 vs 0.161 at $0/20$, 0.0180 vs 0.0146 at
  $0/260$. And it is not the interval that §M3's risk-difference method is
  constructed from; using two different single-proportion intervals in one paper
  would be the worse sin.

## M3. Difference of two independent proportions — Newcombe's hybrid score

### The estimator

Let $(l_1,u_1)$ and $(l_2,u_2)$ be the **Wilson** intervals for $\hat p_1$ and
$\hat p_2$. Newcombe's method 10 sets $\hat\Delta = \hat p_1 - \hat p_2$ and

$$
L = \hat\Delta - \sqrt{(\hat p_1 - l_1)^2 + (u_2 - \hat p_2)^2},
\qquad
U = \hat\Delta + \sqrt{(u_1 - \hat p_1)^2 + (\hat p_2 - l_2)^2}.
$$

The construction is: the interval extends as far as the two score intervals
jointly allow, combining the *nearer* limit of one with the *further* limit of
the other in each direction. It is guaranteed to lie in $[-1,1]$, and because it
is built from Wilson intervals it inherits their behaviour at an empty cell
instead of collapsing.

### Failure modes at this study's $n$

The Wald difference is

$$
\hat\Delta \;\pm\; z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p_1(1-\hat p_1)}{n_1} + \frac{\hat p_2(1-\hat p_2)}{n_2}},
$$

degenerate whenever *both* arms are boundary cells, and under-covering badly
whenever *one* is. Exact coverage, enumerating the product binomial
(`power.py` §11b, nominal 0.95):

| $p_1$ | $p_2$ | $n$/arm | Newcombe | Wald | Wald zero-width |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.30 | 0.05 | 20 | 0.969 | 0.933 | 0.000 |
| 0.30 | 0.05 | 60 | 0.956 | 0.943 | 0.000 |
| 0.10 | 0.01 | 40 | 0.970 | 0.908 | 0.010 |
| 0.05 | 0.00 | 40 | **0.997** | **0.868** | **0.129** |
| 0.50 | 0.30 | 40 | 0.950 | 0.939 | 0.000 |
| 0.02 | 0.02 | 60 | 0.996 | 0.980 | 0.089 |

Newcombe is somewhat **conservative** near the boundary (0.997 where 0.95 is
nominal) — that is the trade, and it is the right direction for a paper whose
headline contrast has an exactly-zero arm.

### Where the paper uses it

$$
\Delta_{\text{inj}} = P(\text{forbidden}\mid\text{attack, delivered})
                    - P(\text{forbidden}\mid\text{clean, delivered}),
$$
$$
\Delta_{\text{safety}} = P(\text{obeyed}\mid\text{benign, delivered})
                       - P(\text{obeyed}\mid\text{attack, delivered}).
$$

Reproduced from the controls run:

| estimand | counts | estimate [Newcombe 95%] | Fisher exact $p$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\Delta_{\text{inj}}$ | 468/1298 vs 0/1297 | **0.361 [0.335, 0.387]** | $8.70\times10^{-164}$ |
| $\Delta_{\text{safety}}$ | 509/1295 vs 468/1298 | **0.032 [−0.005, 0.070]** | 0.089 |

$\Delta_{\text{safety}}$ is the more interesting number and it is **not**
significant: models are not applying a destructive-specific refusal, only a
generic reluctance to act on instructions found in tool output. Note that the
interval, not the $p$-value, is what licenses that reading — it excludes effects
larger than 7pp, which is what makes "no destructive-specific refusal" a claim
rather than a failure to find one.

## M4. Odds ratios and Fisher's exact test

### Fisher's exact test

Conditioning on both margins of

$$
\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix},
\qquad n_1 = a+b,\; n_2 = c+d,\; m = a+c,
$$

the count $A$ is **non-central hypergeometric**:

$$
P(A = a \mid \text{margins}, \omega)
= \frac{\binom{n_1}{a}\binom{n_2}{m-a}\,\omega^{a}}
       {\sum_{j}\binom{n_1}{j}\binom{n_2}{m-j}\,\omega^{j}}.
$$

At $\omega = 1$ this is the central hypergeometric and the two-sided $p$-value is
the total probability of every table whose probability does not exceed the
observed table's. That "as extreme in probability" rule is what `power.py`'s
`fisher_reject_fast` implements directly; it agrees cell-for-cell with
`scipy.stats.fisher_exact` at $n = 12, 20, 25$ and is ~120× faster, which is what
makes the required-$n$ search in §M10 feasible.

### Two different odds ratios — do not swap them

* **Conditional MLE.** $\hat\omega$ maximising the non-central hypergeometric
  likelihood. This is the estimand Fisher's test is about.
* **Unconditional (sample) OR.** $\widehat{\mathrm{OR}} = ad/bc$, with the Woolf
  logit interval
  $$\exp\!\left(\log\widehat{\mathrm{OR}} \pm z\sqrt{\tfrac1a+\tfrac1b+\tfrac1c+\tfrac1d}\right).$$

`analyze.odds_ratio` computes the **unconditional** OR with a Woolf interval.
The two agree closely when no cell is small and diverge at the boundary
(`power.py` §11e):

| table | sample OR [Woolf 95%] | Haldane applied | conditional MLE OR [95%] |
|---|---|---|---|
| 266/399 vs 110/599 | 8.891 [6.630, 11.923] | no | 8.867 [6.560, 12.055] |
| 92/300 vs 110/599 | 1.966 [1.426, 2.710] | no | 1.965 [1.406, 2.743] |
| 0/30 vs 10/30 | 0.032 [0.002, 0.577] | **yes** | 0.000 [0.000, 0.347] |
| 0/20 vs 0/20 | 1.000 [0.019, 52.849] | **yes** | undefined |
| 1/40 vs 0/40 | 3.076 [0.122, 77.796] | **yes** | ∞ [0.026, ∞) |

The first two rows are the study's framing contrasts: `spec_voice` vs
`admin_note` **OR 8.89 [6.63, 11.92]**, $p = 1.68\times10^{-54}$; `html_comment`
vs `admin_note` **OR 1.97 [1.43, 2.71]**, $p = 4.48\times10^{-5}$.

### The Haldane–Anscombe correction

When any cell is zero, $\log \widehat{\mathrm{OR}} = \pm\infty$ and the Woolf
variance $1/a + 1/b + 1/c + 1/d$ diverges. The correction adds $\tfrac12$ to all
four cells:

$$
\widehat{\mathrm{OR}}_{\text{HA}}
= \frac{(a+\tfrac12)(d+\tfrac12)}{(b+\tfrac12)(c+\tfrac12)}.
$$

`analyze.odds_ratio` applies it only when $\min(a,b,c,d) = 0$ and **reports a
flag** saying it did — this appears in the output tables and must appear in the
paper, because the correction biases the estimate toward 1. For a claim that
containment *works* (OR far from 1) that bias is conservative, which is the
direction a referee should want; for a claim that containment does *nothing* it
is anti-conservative and the raw counts must be shown instead.

## M5. Paired data — McNemar, and the paired risk difference

The containment contrast, the defense contrast and the attack-vs-benign contrast
are all **exactly matched pairs**: the two arms share `attack_id` (containment
and condition are deliberately excluded from the hash), share `split`, and share
the decoding seed, since `runner._seed` keys only on `(attack_id, trial_idx)`.
An unpaired analysis of these would throw away that matching.

Arrange the pairs as

|  | B = 1 | B = 0 |
|---|---|---|
| **A = 1** | $a_{11}$ | $b$ |
| **A = 0** | $c$ | $a_{00}$ |

with $n = a_{11}+b+c+a_{00}$.

### M5.1 McNemar's exact conditional test

The concordant cells carry no information about the marginal difference, so the
test conditions on the number of discordant pairs:

$$
b \mid (b+c) \;\sim\; \mathrm{Binomial}\!\left(b+c,\;\tfrac12\right)
\quad\text{under } H_0: p_{10} = p_{01},
$$

and the two-sided $p$-value is

$$
p = \min\!\Big(1,\; 2\,F_{\text{Bin}}\big(\min(b,c);\,b+c,\,\tfrac12\big)\Big).
$$

The asymptotic alternative is $\chi^2 = (b-c)^2/(b+c)$ on 1 df (with or without
a continuity correction).

**When the exact form is required.** The $\chi^2$ approximation needs $b+c$
large; the usual rule of thumb is $b+c \ge 25$. This study routinely produces
single-digit discordance — a hardened defense that drives both arms toward zero
gives $b+c \in \{0,1,2,3\}$ by design. From `power.py` §11d:

| $b$ | $c$ | exact $p$ (formula) | statsmodels exact | `binomtest` | $\chi^2$ approx |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 107 | 148 | 0.0120987390 | 0.0120987390 | 0.0120987390 | 0.0102429001 |
| 25 | 12 | 0.0470310274 | 0.0470310274 | 0.0470310274 | 0.0325827984 |
| 17 | 1 | 0.0001449585 | 0.0001449585 | 0.0001449585 | 0.0001624408 |
| 10 | 0 | 0.0019531250 | 0.0019531250 | 0.0019531250 | 0.0015654023 |
| 3 | 1 | 0.6250000000 | 0.6250000000 | 0.6250000000 | 0.3173105079 |
| 1 | 0 | 1.0000000000 | 1.0000000000 | 1.0000000000 | 0.3173105079 |
| 0 | 0 | 1.0000000000 | 1.0000000000 | 1.0000000000 | 1.0000000000 |

At $b+c = 1$ the $\chi^2$ approximation returns $p = 0.317$ where the exact test
returns $1.000$: a single discordant pair is the **least** possible evidence and
the approximation treats it as a third of the way to significance. At
$b+c = 37$ it is still anti-conservative by 1.4pp. The exact form is required,
not preferred.

**Reproduced end to end.** The paper's attack-vs-benign contrast, recomputed
from `trials.db` by merging on
`(model, quant, defense, attack_id, trial_idx)`:

```

McNemar exact p = 0.01209873900021325   (statsmodels == binomtest == formula)
```

matching the reported $p_{\text{exact}} = 0.0121$.

### M5.2 The paired risk difference, and why Wald is unusable

$$
\widehat{\mathrm{RD}} = \frac{b-c}{n},
\qquad
\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}_{\text{Wald}}(\widehat{\mathrm{RD}})
= \frac{b + c - (b-c)^2/n}{n^2}.
$$

Set $b = c = 0$: the variance is exactly $0$ and the interval is exactly
$[0,0]$. That is not a rare pathology — it is the **expected** output of a
defense that works, and it prints as "0.000 [0.000, 0.000]", a claim that the
effect is known with certainty. Exact coverage in the study's regime
(`power.py` §11c(iii), nominal 0.95, $n = 40$):

| $p_{10}$ | $p_{01}$ | Tango | Wald | Wald is $[0,0]$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.02 | 0.00 | 0.999 | **0.553** | **0.446** |
| 0.03 | 0.00 | 0.999 | 0.703 | 0.296 |
| 0.05 | 0.00 | 0.997 | 0.868 | 0.129 |
| 0.05 | 0.02 | 0.986 | 0.864 | 0.055 |
| 0.10 | 0.02 | 0.966 | 0.910 | 0.006 |
| 0.15 | 0.05 | 0.956 | 0.941 | 0.000 |
| 0.30 | 0.03 | 0.952 | 0.930 | 0.000 |

`analyze.tango_rd`'s docstring quotes "68%" for this regime; the enumeration
locates that at $p_{10}\approx0.03,\;p_{01}=0$. It is one point on a curve
running from 55% to 94%, and the curve is what belongs in the paper.

### M5.3 Tango's asymptotic score interval

Tango inverts a score test for $H_0: \delta = \delta_0$. Let $m = n - b - c$ and
let $\hat q(\delta)$ be the **constrained** MLE of $p_{01}$ under
$p_{10}-p_{01} = \delta$, i.e. the admissible root of

$$
2n\,q^2 \;-\; \Big[(b+c)(1-\delta) - 2\delta\,(c+m)\Big]\,q \;-\; c\,\delta(1-\delta) \;=\; 0 ,
$$

taking the root $q = \big(-B + \sqrt{B^2-4AC}\big)/(2A)$ and clipping it to the
admissible range $[\max(0,-\delta),\;\max((1-\delta)/2,0)]$.

Substituting `analyze._tango_q`'s output back into the left-hand side, over
$(b,c,n)$ in $\{(17,1,60),\,(107,148,1555),\,(10,0,30),\,(3,1,10),\,(25,12,100),\,(0,5,40)\}$
crossed with $\delta$ in $\{-0.3,\,-0.05,\,0,\,0.05,\,0.2,\,0.4\}$, leaves a
maximum residual of $2.8\times10^{-14}$ — the implementation solves this equation
and not a sign variant of it (the same check with $+B$ leaves a residual of 34.3).

The score statistic is

$$
T(\delta) \;=\; \frac{b - c - n\delta}
{\sqrt{\,n\big(2\hat q(\delta) + \delta(1-\delta)\big)\,}},
$$

and the $100(1-\alpha)\%$ interval is the set of $\delta$ with
$|T(\delta)| \le z_{\alpha/2}$. Since $T$ is decreasing in $\delta$, the **lower**
limit solves $T(\delta) = +z$ and the upper solves $T(\delta) = -z$.
`analyze.tango_rd` finds both by bracketed bisection.

**Closed form at $b = c = 0$.** Then $\hat q = 0$ for $\delta > 0$, so
$T(\delta) = -\sqrt{n\delta/(1-\delta)}$, and $T = -z$ gives

$$
\boxed{\;\mathrm{CI} = \left[-\frac{z^2}{n+z^2},\; +\frac{z^2}{n+z^2}\right]\;}
$$

Verified numerically (`power.py` §11c(i)):

| $n$ | `tango_rd(0,0,n)` | $z^2/(n+z^2)$ | Wald |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30 | [−0.113513, +0.113513] | 0.113513 | (0.0, 0.0) |
| 40 | [−0.087622, +0.087622] | 0.087622 | (0.0, 0.0) |
| 260 | [−0.014560, +0.014560] | 0.014560 | (0.0, 0.0) |
| 680 | [−0.005617, +0.005617] | 0.005617 | (0.0, 0.0) |

**The limits really do invert the score test.** Evaluating `analyze._tango_stat`
at the returned limits (`power.py` §11c(ii)):

| $b$ | $c$ | $n$ | $T(\text{lo})$ | $T(\text{hi})$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 17 | 1 | 60 | +1.959964 | −1.959964 |
| 107 | 148 | 1555 | +1.959964 | −1.959964 |
| 10 | 0 | 30 | +1.959964 | −1.959964 |
| 0 | 0 | 40 | +1.959964 | −1.959964 |

against the target $\pm z_{.025} = \pm1.959964$ — to six decimal places, at
every table including the degenerate one.

### M5.4 The conditional odds ratio for paired data

$$
\hat\psi = \frac{b}{c},
$$

with an **exact** interval obtained by transforming a Clopper–Pearson interval
for $\pi = P(\text{discordant pair favours A}) = b/(b+c)$:

$$
\hat\psi = \frac{\hat\pi}{1-\hat\pi},
\qquad
\mathrm{CI}(\psi) = \left[\frac{\pi_L}{1-\pi_L},\;\frac{\pi_U}{1-\pi_U}\right].
$$

For attack-vs-benign, $b = 107$, $c = 148$ gives $\hat\psi = 0.723$ with exact
95% interval $[0.558, 0.933]$ — matching the reported **0.72 [0.56, 0.93]**,
and $\widehat{\mathrm{RD}} = -0.0264$ with Tango interval $[-0.0466, -0.0063]$,
matching **−0.026 [−0.047, −0.006]**.

$\psi$ and RD are **different estimands** and §M9 shows they can disagree about
whether an effect is homogeneous across models. Pick one before the run.

## M6. Logistic regression with cluster-robust standard errors

### The model

$$
\operatorname{logit} P(Y_i = 1) \;=\; \beta_0 + Z_i'\beta,
\qquad
\operatorname{logit}(\pi) = \log\frac{\pi}{1-\pi},
$$

where $Z_i$ holds treatment-coded indicators for the attack factors (`framing`,
`position`, `authority`, `carrier` — plus `defense` in the ITT fit). The
reference level of each factor is its alphabetically first level.
$\exp(\beta_j)$ is an odds ratio against that reference.

### The sandwich

Let $X$ be the $N\times K$ design matrix, $\mu_i$ the fitted probabilities,
$W = \operatorname{diag}\!\big(\mu_i(1-\mu_i)\big)$, and $u_i = y_i - \mu_i$.
For a binomial GLM with the canonical link the score contribution of an
observation is $x_i(y_i-\mu_i)$, so the raw residual is the whole of it. With
clusters $g = 1\ldots G$:

$$
\hat V \;=\; c \cdot (X'WX)^{-1}
\left(\sum_{g=1}^{G} X_g' u_g u_g' X_g\right)
(X'WX)^{-1},
$$

the classic bread–meat–bread of Liang and Zeger. The finite-cluster correction
`statsmodels` applies by default is

$$
c \;=\; \frac{G}{G-1}\cdot\frac{N-1}{N-K}.
$$

**Verified by hand** on the study's own delivered-attack sample
(`power.py` §11f), $N = 1298$, $G = 5$, $K = 3$:

| | Intercept | html_comment | spec_voice |
|---|---|---|---|
| hand SE, no correction | 0.355314939 | 0.561241936 | 0.333700249 |
| hand SE, $c = 1.251931$ | **0.397560821** | **0.627971919** | **0.373376209** |
| `statsmodels res.bse` | **0.397560821** | **0.627971919** | **0.373376209** |

The corrected hand computation reproduces `statsmodels` exactly, which is the
point: the reported standard errors are the formula above and nothing else.

### Why not a mixed model

`analyze.logistic_cluster` is deliberately *not* a random-intercept GLMM. With
$G \approx 6$ model clusters the random-intercept variance is estimated from six
draws: badly biased, with a meaningless standard error, and Laplace/AGQ
likelihoods for binary outcomes with tiny cluster counts are unreliable on top
of that. The cluster-robust GLM makes no distributional assumption about
model-level heterogeneity — only the mean model has to be right.

### The small-$G$ problem, quantitatively

The sandwich is consistent as $G\to\infty$ and **downward-biased** at small $G$.
Simulation (`power.py` §15d; binomial GLM, treatment varying *within* cluster,
true effect zero, cluster random intercept with ICC 0.10, 400 sims per row):

| $G$ | FPR with normal critical values | FPR with $t(G-1)$ | $t(G-1)/z$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 0.160 | 0.048 | 1.62 |
| 5 | **0.113** | **0.045** | 1.42 |
| 6 | 0.102 | 0.035 | 1.31 |
| 10 | 0.083 | 0.070 | 1.15 |
| 30 | 0.048 | 0.043 | 1.04 |

At $G = 5$ a nominal 5% test rejects 11% of the time with normal critical
values. Using $t(G-1)$ restores nominal size at the cost of intervals **41.7%
wider**. That is why `analyze.py` uses $t(G-1)$, and the widening is a real
price, not a formality.

Two caveats that must travel with this table:

1. **This is the favourable case.** The treatment varies *within* cluster. A
   model-level covariate (RQ1/RQ2's capability score) has $G$ effective
   observations and no critical-value correction rescues it — see §M8.
2. **`deepseek-r1` contributes no delivered trials**, so the delivered-attack
   fit runs at $G = 5$, not 6. That is one fewer cluster than the model count
   suggests, and it changes the critical value from $t(5) = 2.571$ to
   $t(4) = 2.776$.

### The failure $t(G-1)$ does **not** fix: $K > G$

The meat is a sum of $G$ outer products of a $K$-vector, so

$$
\operatorname{rank}\!\left(\sum_g X_g' u_g u_g' X_g\right) \;\le\; G,
$$

and because the total score vanishes at the MLE, the achievable rank is
$G-1$. When $K > G$, $\hat V$ is **singular**: there are $K-(G-1)$ directions in
parameter space with *exactly zero* estimated variance. The ablation stage
crosses `framing` × `position` × `authority` × `carrier`, giving
$K = 1+2+2+2+3 = 10$ against $G = 6$. Simulated (`power.py` §15e):

| $G$ | $K$ | rank$(\hat V)$ | min eigenvalue | max SE | `analyze.py` flags it? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 10 | 3 | $-2.7\times10^{-17}$ | 0.253 | no |
| 5 | 10 | 4 | $-1.5\times10^{-17}$ | 0.210 | no |
| 6 | 10 | 5 | $-7.5\times10^{-18}$ | 0.170 | no |

A contrast lying in that null space has

```
estimate +0.576794    SE 0.000e+00    |t| = inf    p = 0
```

while every individual coefficient standard error still looks entirely normal
(max 0.170). `analyze.logistic_cluster`'s separation guard tests for non-finite
or absurdly large standard errors and would **not** catch this. A rank check on
`res.cov_params()` would, and this is an open item (§M12).

### Separation

If any factor level has a 0% or 100% obedience rate, the MLE does not exist:
coefficients diverge and their standard errors are arbitrary. `analyze.py`
detects this — requiring an actually-separated level *and* an extreme
coefficient or fitted value, so that a genuinely large effect on a rare outcome
is not misdiagnosed — and **suppresses the whole table**, not just the separated
row, because no coefficient in a diverged fit is interpretable. The fallback is
§4c: Fisher exact tests with Newcombe intervals, which are valid at zero cells.

### The clustering that is *not* modelled

Twenty trials on one `attack_id` share a prompt and differ only by decoding
seed, so attack instance is a second, crossed clustering. `analyze.py` reports
both `model`-clustered and `attack_id`-clustered standard errors side by side
rather than choosing the prettier one. The design-effect consequence
($\mathrm{DE} = 1 + (m-1)\rho$, effective $n = km/\mathrm{DE}$, ceiling
$k/\rho$ as $m\to\infty$) is `power.py` §4, and its conclusion — more attack
instances beat more trials per instance — stands.

## M7. Multiplicity — Holm's step-down procedure

### The rule

Order the $m$ $p$-values $p_{(1)} \le p_{(2)} \le \cdots \le p_{(m)}$ with
hypotheses $H_{(1)},\ldots,H_{(m)}$. Let

$$
k \;=\; \min\left\{\, i \;:\; p_{(i)} > \frac{\alpha}{m-i+1} \,\right\}
$$

and reject $H_{(1)},\ldots,H_{(k-1)}$ (all of them, if no such $k$ exists).
Equivalently, in adjusted-$p$ form,

$$
\tilde p_{(i)} \;=\; \max_{j\le i}\;\min\Big(1,\;(m-j+1)\,p_{(j)}\Big),
$$

which is monotone by construction and is what `analyze.holm` returns.

### Why it controls FWER with no independence assumption

Let $I_0$ be the set of true nulls, $m_0 = |I_0| \ge 1$, and suppose a true null
is rejected. Let $H_{(i^\ast)}$ be the *first* true null rejected in the
step-down order. Every hypothesis rejected before it is false, so at most
$m - m_0$ of them, hence $i^\ast \le m - m_0 + 1$ and therefore
$m - i^\ast + 1 \ge m_0$. Rejection requires
$p_{(i^\ast)} \le \alpha/(m-i^\ast+1) \le \alpha/m_0$. So

$$
\mathrm{FWER} \;\le\; P\!\left(\min_{i \in I_0} p_i \le \frac{\alpha}{m_0}\right)
\;\le\; \sum_{i\in I_0} P\!\left(p_i \le \frac{\alpha}{m_0}\right)
\;=\; m_0 \cdot \frac{\alpha}{m_0} \;=\; \alpha .
$$

The only step is Boole's inequality, which needs no independence and no
positive-dependence condition. That is the whole reason Holm rather than
Benjamini–Hochberg is used here: the framing contrasts share a common reference
arm and are strongly positively correlated, and BH's FDR guarantee requires a
dependence condition that would have to be argued.

### Verification and behaviour under dependence

`analyze.holm` against the closed-form rule, 2000 random $p$-vectors of length
2–11 (`power.py` §11g):

```
max |analyze.holm - closed-form rule| = 0.00e+00
analyze.holm([0.01, nan, 0.04]) = [0.02, nan, 0.04]
```

NaN $p$-values are dropped from the family, so $m$ is the number of **estimable**
tests. That is the right convention — an inestimable contrast is not a hypothesis
that was tested and survived — but it makes $m$ data-dependent, and $m$ must be
reported alongside the adjusted values.

FWER under the global null with 21 pairwise framing tests and exchangeable
correlation $\rho$ between the $z$ statistics (`power.py` §15c, 40 000 sims):

| $\rho$ | uncorrected | Bonferroni | Holm | mean rejections |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | 0.6574 | 0.0500 | 0.0500 | 0.052 |
| 0.30 | 0.5547 | 0.0442 | 0.0442 | 0.051 |
| 0.60 | 0.3628 | 0.0308 | 0.0308 | 0.056 |
| 0.90 | 0.1557 | 0.0112 | 0.0112 | 0.087 |
| 0.99 | 0.0751 | 0.0041 | 0.0041 | 0.070 |

Uncorrected, 21 tests reject *something* two thirds of the time under a true
global null. Holm never exceeds $\alpha$ at any dependence. Under the global
null Holm's first step *is* Bonferroni, so the two coincide exactly; Holm's gain
appears under partial nulls, where it keeps rejecting after the first. Under
strong positive dependence both become conservative — the price of assuming
nothing.

**Families in this study** (each corrected within itself, never across):

* framing contrasts against the reference framing;
* the ITT McNemar hypotheses — the `[both delivered]` rows are perfectly
  correlated re-analyses of the same contrast, not separate tests, and are
  reported uncorrected and marked descriptive.

## M8. Rank correlation across models

### Spearman's $\rho$ and the test actually run

$\rho_S$ is Pearson's correlation of the ranks. `analyze.spearman_exact`
enumerates all $n!$ orderings for $n \le 8$ and reports the exact permutation
$p$-value

$$
p \;=\; \frac{\#\{\pi \in S_n : |\rho_S(x, y_\pi)| \ge |\rho_S^{\text{obs}}|\}}{n!},
$$

falling back to the asymptotic $p$-value only for $n > 8$. So the relevant null
is **discrete**, and its granularity — not its variance — is what binds.

The reported interval is Fisher's $z$ transform with the conventional inflation
factor of 1.06 that adapts the product-moment standard error to a *rank*
correlation. **The 1.06 inflates the VARIANCE, not the standard error** —
$\operatorname{Var}(z) \approx 1.06/(n-3)$ — so it sits inside the square root:

$$
z = \operatorname{artanh}(\hat\rho),\quad
\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{1.06}{n-3}} \;=\; \frac{1.0296}{\sqrt{n-3}},\quad
\mathrm{CI} = \tanh\!\big(z \pm z_{\alpha/2}\,\mathrm{SE}\big).
$$

Writing $1.06/\sqrt{n-3}$ instead inflates the SE by a further factor of
$\sqrt{1.06} = 1.0296$, i.e. ~3% uniformly. Monte Carlo (bivariate normal,
20 000 sims, SD of $\operatorname{artanh}\hat\rho_S$) locates the correct form:

| $n$ | realised SD ($\rho{=}0$) | $\sqrt{1.06/(n-3)}$ | $1.06/\sqrt{n-3}$ | $1/\sqrt{n-3}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 0.2427 | **0.2497** | 0.2571 | 0.2425 |
| 40 | 0.1638 | **0.1693** | 0.1743 | 0.1644 |
| 80 | 0.1140 | **0.1173** | 0.1208 | 0.1140 |

The error was conservative — every interval was ~3% too wide — so no reported
conclusion flips, but the quoted limits were wrong and are now recomputed.

This interval and the permutation $p$-value are **not** dual: the interval is
asymptotic and the test is exact, so an interval excluding 0 alongside
$p > 0.05$ is possible at these $n$. Where they disagree, the exact test is the
one to believe, and the interval should be read as a rough width statement.

### The floor at six models

## M9. Heterogeneity — and why "model-dependent" is not scale-free

The containment headline ("a real mitigation for one model, nearly worthless for
another") is a **heterogeneity** claim. Cochran's $Q$ tests it:

$$
Q \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{G} w_i\,(\hat\theta_i - \bar\theta)^2,
\qquad
w_i = \frac{1}{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\theta_i)},
\qquad
\bar\theta = \frac{\sum_i w_i \hat\theta_i}{\sum_i w_i},
$$

with $Q \sim \chi^2_{G-1}$ under homogeneity, and
$I^2 = \max\!\big(0, (Q-(G-1))/Q\big)$ as the descriptive companion.

But $\theta$ has to be *something*, and here the answer changes with the choice.
The two candidates are

$$
\theta^{\mathrm{RD}}_i = \frac{b_i - c_i}{n_i},
\qquad
\widehat{\mathrm{Var}} = \frac{b_i + c_i - (b_i-c_i)^2/n_i}{n_i^2},
$$

$$
\theta^{\mathrm{OR}}_i = \log\frac{b_i + \tfrac12}{c_i + \tfrac12},
\qquad
\widehat{\mathrm{Var}} = \frac{1}{b_i+\tfrac12} + \frac{1}{c_i+\tfrac12} .
$$

Simulated rejection rates for the same data-generating process analysed on each
scale — five models with the study's measured concatenated baselines,
$\phi = 0.5$, 4000 sims per cell (`power.py` §14):

| scenario | $n$ | $Q$ on RD | $Q$ on log-OR |
|---|---|---|---|
| **NULL on RD**: every model loses exactly 0.10 | 170 | **0.053** | 0.297 |
| | 340 | **0.051** | 0.881 |
| | 680 | **0.050** | **1.000** |
| **NULL on the odds scale**: $\psi = 4$ for every model | 170 | 0.959 | **0.025** |
| | 340 | 1.000 | **0.034** |
| | 680 | **1.000** | **0.039** |
| **NULL on relative reduction**: $r = 0.50$ for every model | 170 | 1.000 | 0.264 |
| | 340 | 1.000 | 0.696 |
| | 680 | 1.000 | 0.979 |
| ALT probe-shaped: $r = 1.0/.75/.5/.5/.375$ | 170 | 1.000 | 0.518 |
| | 340 | 1.000 | 0.976 |
| | 680 | 1.000 | 1.000 |
| ALT on the odds scale: $\psi = 50/20/4/3/1.6$ | 170 | 0.449 | 0.790 |
| | 340 | 0.767 | 1.000 |
| | 680 | 0.976 | 1.000 |

Read the two NULL blocks first. $Q$ on the risk-difference scale holds its size
(0.050–0.053) when the risk difference is constant and rejects **100%** of the
time when the *odds* ratio is constant. $Q$ on the log-odds scale does the mirror
image. At $n$ in the hundreds neither is robust to the wrong choice, and the
containment stage will produce $n = 680$ pairs per model.

Note also that a **constant relative reduction** — arguably the most natural
informal reading of "containment works the same for every model" — is neither
null: it is rejected at 100% on the RD scale and 97.9% on the log-OR scale at
$n = 680$. Models with a high concatenated baseline mechanically show a larger
absolute reduction, and a fixed marginal ratio does not fix the conditional
odds ratio.

**This is a pre-registration item, not a footnote.** The scale must be fixed
before the run. The recommendation:

* **Primary: the conditional odds ratio $\psi = p_{10}/p_{01}$**, because it is
  the estimand McNemar actually tests and it is what the exact conditional
  interval in §M5.4 covers.
* **Reported beside it: the risk difference**, because that is what a deployment
  decision needs — a 30pp absolute reduction and a 3pp absolute reduction are
  not the same recommendation even at the same odds ratio.
* **Not defensible: choosing after the run.** At this $n$ both are available and
  they disagree by construction, so a post-hoc choice is a choice of conclusion.

## M10. Power

Power calculations here are stated as equations and then **checked against exact
enumeration and against Monte Carlo**, because the closed forms are normal
approximations and the tests actually run are discrete.

### M10.1 Two independent proportions

Power of the pooled two-sample $z$ test, using the null variance in the
rejection rule and the alternative variance in the distribution of the
statistic:

$$
1-\beta \;=\; \Phi\!\left(
\frac{|p_1-p_2| - z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{2\bar p(1-\bar p)/n}}
     {\sqrt{\big(p_1(1-p_1)+p_2(1-p_2)\big)/n}}\right)
+ \Phi\!\left(
\frac{-|p_1-p_2| - z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{2\bar p(1-\bar p)/n}}
     {\sqrt{\big(p_1(1-p_1)+p_2(1-p_2)\big)/n}}\right),
$$

with $\bar p = (p_1+p_2)/2$; inverting for $n$ per arm,

$$
\boxed{\;
n \;=\; \frac{\Big[z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{2\bar p(1-\bar p)}
             + z_{\beta}\sqrt{p_1(1-p_1)+p_2(1-p_2)}\Big]^2}{(p_1-p_2)^2}
\;}
$$

Cohen's arcsine form, quoted in `DESIGN.md`, is
$n = 2(z_{\alpha/2}+z_\beta)^2/h^2$ with
$h = 2\arcsin\sqrt{p_1} - 2\arcsin\sqrt{p_2}$.

Fisher's exact test is conditional and discrete and needs more. The
Casagrande–Pike–Smith continuity correction bridges them:

$$
n_{\mathrm{CPS}} \;=\; \frac{n}{4}\left[1 + \sqrt{1 + \frac{4}{n\,|p_1-p_2|}}\right]^2 .
$$

**The constant is 4.** With equal arms of size $n$ the Yates correction to
$|\hat p_1 - \hat p_2|$ is $N/(2n_1n_2) = 1/n$; solving
$\sqrt{n'}\,(\Delta - 1/n') = \Delta\sqrt{n}$ for $n'$ gives the form above
exactly. An earlier revision of this appendix and of `power.py` carried an 8,
which over-inflates every cell. Two checks, both by execution:

* against the $n$ Fisher's exact test actually requires, mean $|n_{\mathrm{CPS}}
  - n_{\text{Fisher}}|$ is **2.96** at $k=4$ and **14.38** at $k=8$;
* simulating the power of the continuity-corrected $z$ test *at* the CPS $n$
  (target 0.80) gives 0.804–0.819 at $k=4$ and 0.846–0.867 at $k=8$ — the 8-form
  is not a bridge to Fisher, it is an over-shoot past it.

| 0.115 | 0.604 | 50 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 0.0000 | 1.000 |

The last row is the **size** check: under the null both the enumeration and the

### M10.2 The paired (McNemar) case

**Power depends on the discordant probabilities and nothing else.** The
marginals do not enter; two designs with identical marginal rates and different
within-pair correlation need different $n$. With
$\delta = p_{10}-p_{01}$ and $p_d = p_{10}+p_{01}$:

$$
1-\beta \;\approx\; \Phi\!\left(
\frac{\sqrt{n}\,|\delta| - z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{p_d}}
     {\sqrt{p_d - \delta^2}}\right),
\qquad
\boxed{\;
n \;=\; \frac{\Big[z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{p_d} + z_\beta\sqrt{p_d-\delta^2}\Big]^2}{\delta^2}
\;}
$$

Connor's parameterisation in terms of the discordance ratio
$\psi = p_{10}/p_{01}$,

$$
n \;=\; \frac{\Big[z_{\alpha/2}(\psi+1) + z_\beta\sqrt{(\psi+1)^2 - (\psi-1)^2 p_d}\Big]^2}
              {(\psi-1)^2\,p_d},
$$

is the **same equation**, obtained by substituting $\delta = p_d(\psi-1)/(\psi+1)$.
Checked numerically (`power.py` §12b):

```
p10=0.10 p01=0.02:  Miettinen 144.786021   Connor 144.786021   |diff| 2.84e-14
p10=0.20 p01=0.05:  Miettinen  84.813016   Connor  84.813016   |diff| 1.42e-14
p10=0.15 p01=0.10:  Miettinen 782.525955   Connor 782.525955   |diff| 4.55e-13
```

The exact power is computed by enumeration, not by this formula:
$D \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, p_d)$, and given $D = d$, $b \sim \mathrm{Bin}(d, q)$
with $q = p_{01}/p_d$; sum the exact-test rejection probability over $d$.
Required pairs (`power.py` §12b):

| $p_{10}$ | $p_{01}$ | $p_d$ | $\delta$ | closed $n_{80}$ | exact $n_{80}$ | closed $n_{90}$ | exact $n_{90}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.10 | 0.02 | 0.12 | 0.08 | 144.8 | 153 | 192.8 | 197 |
| 0.20 | 0.05 | 0.25 | 0.15 | 84.8 | 92 | 112.5 | 119 |
| 0.30 | 0.01 | 0.31 | 0.29 | 26.4 | 28 | 34.4 | 33 |
| 0.05 | 0.01 | 0.06 | 0.04 | 292.0 | 338 | 389.9 | 471 |
| 0.15 | 0.10 | 0.25 | 0.05 | 782.5 | 892 | 1046.6 | 1219 |
| 0.30 | 0.00 | 0.30 | 0.30 | 23.7 | 25 | 30.6 | 29 |
| 0.25 | 0.05 | 0.30 | 0.20 | 56.4 | 61 | 74.6 | 78 |

**Where the closed form fails** (`power.py` §15b, 20 000 sims):

| $p_{10}$ | $p_{01}$ | $n$ | closed | exact | MC | \|exact−MC\| | closed error |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.100 | 0.020 | 60 | 0.430 | **0.281** | 0.281 | 0.0002 | **+0.149** |
| 0.200 | 0.050 | 40 | 0.474 | 0.364 | 0.363 | 0.0006 | +0.110 |
| 0.300 | 0.010 | 30 | 0.852 | 0.853 | 0.853 | 0.0004 | −0.000 |
| 0.050 | 0.010 | 200 | 0.638 | 0.562 | 0.559 | 0.0032 | +0.076 |
| 0.150 | 0.100 | 260 | 0.363 | 0.322 | 0.320 | 0.0017 | +0.041 |
| 0.294 | 0.000 | 30 | 0.885 | 0.913 | 0.913 | 0.0004 | −0.028 |
| **0.020** | **0.020** | **200** | **0.050** | **0.018** | **0.020** | 0.0016 | +0.032 |

Exact and Monte Carlo agree to Monte-Carlo error everywhere, which validates the
enumeration. The closed form **overstates** power by up to 0.149 in the
rare-discordance regime and *understates* it when discordance is one-sided and
large ($p_{01} = 0$).

The mechanism is the discreteness of the conditional null. With $d = b+c$
discordant pairs the smallest attainable two-sided $p$-value is $2^{-(d-1)}$, so
(`power.py` §15b)

| $d$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| min attainable $p$ | 1.0000 | 0.5000 | 0.2500 | 0.1250 | 0.0625 | 0.0312 | 0.0156 | 0.0078 |

**Fewer than six discordant pairs can never reject at $\alpha = 0.05$, no matter
how one-sided they are.** The last row of the table is the size check and shows
the consequence: nominal 0.05, actual 0.018, because at $n = 200$ and
$p_d = 0.04$ a fraction $P(d \le 5) = 0.186$ of samples is incapable of
rejecting before the data are even looked at. This is also why a hardened
defense that drives both arms to near-zero can be a real defense and still
produce $p > 0.05$: read the Tango interval, not the $p$-value.

**Plan from the enumeration.** The closed forms are here because the appendix
must state them, not because they should be used.

### M10.3 Correlation

For Fisher's $z$, $\operatorname{artanh}(\hat r)$ is approximately normal with
standard error $1/\sqrt{n-3}$, giving

$$
1-\beta = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\operatorname{artanh}(r) - z_{\alpha/2}/\sqrt{n-3}}{1/\sqrt{n-3}}\right)
+ \Phi\!\left(\frac{-\operatorname{artanh}(r) - z_{\alpha/2}/\sqrt{n-3}}{1/\sqrt{n-3}}\right),
$$

and a smallest detectable $|r| = \tanh\!\big(z_{\alpha/2}/\sqrt{n-3}\big)$. As
§M8 shows, at $n \le 8$ this is the wrong test and the wrong answer; use the
exact permutation critical values.

## M11. The containment stage

### M11.1 The factor

`containment` $\in$ {`concatenated`, `contained`}:

* **concatenated** — the payload is appended or prepended **raw** to the
  serialized tool result. This models a poisoned upstream source whose text a
  wrapper passes through without re-encoding.
* **contained** — the identical payload text sits inside a well-formed record of
  the carrier's own type, JSON-encoded so that quote, backslash and newline are
  escaped. The payload cannot emit the closing delimiter, because the delimiter
  is produced by the encoder and not by the text.

`containment` is **not** in the `attack_id` hash (exactly as `condition` is not),
so the two arms of a cell share an `attack_id`, share a `split`, and share a
decoding seed. The pairs are **exact**, not merely balanced — which is what
makes §M5 available rather than §M3.

### M11.2 The estimand

For each model $m$, on pairs delivered in **both** arms:

$$
\theta_m^{\mathrm{RD}} = P(\text{obeyed}\mid\text{concat}) - P(\text{obeyed}\mid\text{contained}),
\qquad
\psi_m = \frac{p_{10}^{(m)}}{p_{01}^{(m)}} .
$$

The pooled-over-models effect is **not** the primary quantity: the probe found
the effect to be model-dependent, so a pooled estimate averages a mitigation
that works with one that does not. Per-model estimates plus a homogeneity test
(§M9) are primary; any pooled figure is a summary, is reported with $I^2$, and
is named as such.

**Conditioning on delivery is stronger here than anywhere else in the study.**
For `framing` the identification argument is that the payload is invisible until
after the carrier call, and §M0's flatness check is what makes it falsifiable.
For `containment` the two arms are *byte-identical at turn 1* — same system
prompt, same user prompt, same seed — so the factor cannot influence delivery
even in principle. Any delivery discordance observed between the arms is
therefore pure run-to-run nondeterminism (Ollama and GPU kernels are not
bit-reproducible across invocations), and its magnitude is itself worth
reporting: it is a free measurement of the noise floor that every same-seed
claim in the paper rests on.

### M11.3 The unbalanced design, and why it is unbalanced on purpose

`contained` requires a serialization to be contained *in*. Three carriers have
one — `web_search`, `product_kg`, `get_stock_quote` are JSON-shaped — and
`read_file` does not: its body is newline-joined plain text with no escaping and
no record boundary, so there is nothing for the payload to be subordinate to.

The decision recorded in `DESIGN.md` is to **restrict the containment factor to
structured carriers** rather than invent a plain-text analogue. A plain-text
"container" would have to be a delimiter the payload could itself emit, which is
precisely the property containment is defined by the absence of; it would test a
different construct wearing the same name.

The consequences for the analysis are stated so they cannot be forgotten:

1. **The containment effect is a structured-carrier estimand.** It is never
   marginalized over `read_file`. Writing "containment reduces obedience by X"
   without the qualifier would be averaging over a cell where the factor does not
   exist.
2. **Carrier main effects must not be pooled across containment arms**, because
   the arms are available for different carrier sets. Any carrier comparison is
   reported *within* a containment arm, or directly standardized to a fixed
   containment mix,
   $$
   \hat p^{\text{std}}_{\text{carrier}} = \sum_{a\in\{\text{concat},\text{contained}\}} w_a\,\hat p_{\text{carrier},a},
   \qquad \sum_a w_a = 1,
   $$
   with $w$ fixed in advance and printed with the estimate.
3. **`contained` is undefined, not null.** A missing cell is not a zero and not
   an average. The stage listing in `runner.py` prints
   `(UNBALANCED: no contained arm for read_file — undefined, not null)`, and the
   analysis must carry the same distinction into its tables.

## M12. What this appendix does **not** justify

Stated plainly, because a referee will find these anyway.

1. **Trial-level independence is assumed by every interval here and is false.**
   Twenty trials share one attack string. Wilson and Newcombe intervals on a
   pooled cell are anti-conservative by roughly
   $\sqrt{1+(m-1)\rho_{\text{ICC}}}$. `power.py` §4 quantifies it; the fix is
   more attack instances, not more trials, and that is a design change.
2. **The cluster-robust covariance is singular whenever $K > G$**, and
   `analyze.py`'s current guard does not detect it (§M6). Any joint statement
   about the ablation stage's factors is affected. A rank check on
   `res.cov_params()` is the fix and is not yet implemented.
3. **RQ2's correlation is not estimable on the current model set** (§M8), and no
   amount of trials changes that.
4. **Invalid trials are excluded and their missingness is not random** —
   invalidity is caused by reasoning models overrunning `num_predict`, which is a
   model property. The bounding sensitivity analysis (best-case and worst-case
   imputation) is specified in `DESIGN.md` and belongs beside every headline
   number. On the controls run the invalid rate is 8/4680 = 0.17%, concentrated
   on `deepseek-r1` (7 of 8), so the bounds are tight here — but the ablation
   and defense stages will not be so lucky.
5. **Register and payload length are collinear by construction.** No estimator in
   this appendix separates them; only a length-matched stimulus set would.
6. **The probe's absolute rates do not transfer to the stage** (§M11.5). The
   power projections in §M11.6 use measured concatenated baselines for that
   reason, but $r$ and $\phi$ remain assumptions, and the $\phi = 0$ column is
   included so the reader can see the conservative case.
7. **Every "0.000" and "1.000" printed as a power is a rounding of something
   below 1.** Exact enumeration returns probabilities, not certainties.

## M13. The pre-registered (model × attack_id) cluster bootstrap

**Provenance note, because it breaks this document's own rule stated in the
preamble ("everything numeric below is printed by `power.py`").** This section
is the one exception, by design: it was implemented in `analyze.py` (the file
that already runs the pairing, the GLM fits and the exact McNemar tests this
section builds on), not in `power.py`, so that the bootstrap draws on the exact
same pairing and scope-restriction code the report tables use rather than a
parallel reimplementation that could silently drift from it. Every number
below is regenerated by `python -X utf8 analyze.py --selftest` (checks 28–29)
or `python -X utf8 analyze.py --run-id <controls-heldout|containment-heldout>
--split heldout` (the "4e. Pre-registered (model x attack_id) cluster
bootstrap" section of the printed report). Nothing here is quoted from memory.

### M13.1 Why the resampling unit is (model, attack_id), not trial or model

§M6 already states the diagnosis this section answers: trials are not
independent at two different grains simultaneously, and neither existing
interval respects both at once.

* **The trial-level interval** (Newcombe RD, Fisher/Haldane OR — §M3, §M4)
  treats every trial as an independent draw. It is not: 20 trials repeat one
  `attack_id` against one model, differing only by decoding seed.
* **The model-clustered sandwich** (§M6) gets the *variable* right — model is
  the coarser, more defensible grouping — but is a large-$G$ asymptotic
  approximation, and $G \in \{5, 6\}$ here. §M6's own simulation shows the
  sandwich anti-conservative at small $G$ even with the $t(G-1)$ correction,
  and §M6 additionally shows it going **singular** whenever the parameter
  count exceeds $G-1$, which the framing interaction fit already hits (Table 7
  in `PAPER.md` §7.5 carries exactly this sandwich, at $G=5$).

The design crosses every `attack_id` against every model — confirmed directly
against `trials.db`: `controls-heldout` has 13 attack instances × 6 models =
78 non-empty (model, attack_id) cells of 60 trials each (20 per condition);
`containment-heldout` has 34 × 5 = 170 cells of 40 trials each (20 per
containment arm). Framing, carrier and the other payload-construction factors
are **constant within an `attack_id`** — confirmed the same way (every
`attack_id` in `controls-heldout` has exactly one framing across its 120
trials) — so resampling (model, attack_id) blocks automatically carries a
cluster's framing/carrier/authority assignment along with it, rather than
requiring a separate stratification variable.

Resampling **trials** ignores both dependencies. Resampling **model alone**
(6 clusters, one block of ~780 trials each) discards the attack-level
dependence — trials sharing an `attack_id` inside one model's block would
still be treated as freely exchangeable with trials from a different
`attack_id` in the same block, which they are not: they share a prompt.
Resampling **(model, attack_id) jointly** is the coarsest grouping under which
two trials in *different* cells are plausibly independent (different model,
different attack, or both) and two trials in the *same* cell are never assumed
to be — which is exactly the assumption the trial-level interval makes and the
one this section exists to stop making.

### M13.2 The algorithm

Let $\mathcal{C} = \{c_1, \ldots, c_G\}$ be the set of realised (model,
attack_id) cells present in the sample, each $c_g$ holding the $n_g$ trials
that share that model and that attack instance ($n_g \in \{20, 40, 60\}$
above, depending on run and estimand). Let $\hat\theta = T(\mathcal{C})$ be the
estimator of interest — a risk difference (`_est_delta_inj`,
`_est_delta_safety`) or a Haldane-consistent conditional log-odds ratio
(`_est_framing_or`, `_est_containment_or`) in `analyze.py`; the algorithm below
does not care which.

```
INPUT: clusters {c_1 .. c_G}, estimator T, replicate count B, seed s, level alpha
theta_hat <- T(c_1, ..., c_G)                          # point estimate, full sample
rng <- Generator(seed = s)
for b in 1 .. B:
    draw G indices i_1 .. i_G, EACH UNIFORM ON {1..G}, WITH REPLACEMENT   # cluster resample
    theta*_b <- T(c_{i_1}, ..., c_{i_G})                # every trial in a drawn cluster travels with it
percentile CI  <- [ quantile(theta*, alpha/2), quantile(theta*, 1-alpha/2) ]
for g in 1 .. G:
    theta~_g <- T(c_1, ..., c_{g-1}, c_{g+1}, ..., c_G)  # leave-one-CLUSTER-out jackknife
z0    <- Phi^-1( #{b : theta*_b < theta_hat} / B )
a-hat <- [ sum_g (theta~bar - theta~_g)^3 ] / ( 6 * [ sum_g (theta~bar - theta~_g)^2 ]^1.5 )
for each tail level z in {Phi^-1(alpha/2), Phi^-1(1-alpha/2)}:
    p(z) <- Phi( z0 + (z0+z) / (1 - a-hat*(z0+z)) )
BCa CI <- [ quantile(theta*, p(Phi^-1(alpha/2))), quantile(theta*, p(Phi^-1(1-alpha/2))) ]
OUTPUT: theta_hat, percentile CI, BCa CI
```

This is `analyze.cluster_bootstrap`. Two implementation points that are easy
to get wrong silently:

1. **A drawn cluster is resampled WHOLESALE.** Every trial belonging to a
   drawn (model, attack_id) cell enters the replicate together, with the
   original within-cluster structure (the paired contained/concatenated trials
   for the same `attack_id`, or the attack/clean/benign trials for the same
   `attack_id`) intact — resampling is over $g \in \{1,\ldots,G\}$, never over
   individual rows. When a cluster is drawn twice, its 20/40/60 trials all
   enter the replicate twice.
2. **The jackknife is leave-one-CLUSTER-out**, not leave-one-trial-out, for
   the same reason the bootstrap resamples clusters: the acceleration constant
   $\hat a$ has to measure how $\hat\theta$ responds to removing one
   *independent* unit, and a trial is not one.

**Percentile vs. BCa.** The plain percentile interval is exact only if the
bootstrap distribution of $\hat\theta - \theta$ is both unbiased (median at
zero) and its shape is stable under the true parameter value (no skew that
changes with $\theta$). Neither holds in general for a ratio estimator like a
conditional OR, which is bounded below by 0 and whose sampling distribution is
right-skewed exactly where this study's containment effect is strongest
(gemma4:26b, §M13.4 below). BCa corrects for both: $\hat z_0$ shifts the
percentile levels to fix a median bias (visible directly as a bootstrap
distribution whose median is not at $\hat\theta$), and $\hat a$ additionally
stretches or compresses them to track how the estimator's *variance* itself
changes with $\theta$ (visible as an interval that is not symmetric around a
point that a normal-based interval would center it on). At $\hat a = 0$ and
$\hat z_0 = 0$ the two formulas coincide exactly — BCa is percentile with two
corrections switched on, not a different method.

A percentile and a BCa interval that **agree** (`analyze._boot_divergence_note`
flags a width ratio outside $[2/3, 1.5]$ as material) is itself informative: it
says the correction terms are small, i.e. the bootstrap distribution is close
to symmetric and unbiased on the scale the estimator is being reported on.
Where they **disagree**, prefer BCa — it is the interval with the stated
second-order accuracy property, the plain percentile interval has none.

**Transformation note.** BCa is applied here directly on the natural scale of
each estimator — risk differences in $[-1,1]$, odds ratios in $(0,\infty)$ —
rather than first mapping the OR estimators to log-odds and back. This is a
deliberate use of a documented equivariance property of the BCa construction
[CITE: BCa bootstrap interval, transformation-respecting property, original],
not an oversight: because the percentile levels are what get adjusted (via
$\hat z_0, \hat a$) rather than the raw $\alpha/2, 1-\alpha/2$ quantiles
themselves, applying BCa to $g(\hat\theta)$ for any monotone $g$ and
back-transforming reproduces the same interval endpoints as applying it to
$\hat\theta$ directly, up to the Monte Carlo noise of finite $B$ — unlike a
percentile interval or a normal-approximation interval, both of which are
scale-dependent. Both scales are exercised in the applied results below
(RD-scale for $\Delta_{\text{inj}}$ / $\Delta_{\text{safety}}$ in
`controls-heldout`, OR-scale for the framing and containment quantities in
both runs), and neither shows the width-ratio warning associated with a
poorly-behaved scale choice.

### M13.3 Numerical validation: coverage under known cluster-correlated ground truth

The standard this file already holds itself to for every other interval (§M2's
Wilson-vs-`statsmodels` check, §M6's hand-computed sandwich SE) is: does the
implementation reproduce a number that can be checked independently. A
bootstrap CI has no closed form to check against, so the check has to be a
**coverage simulation** — generate data with a KNOWN parameter under the same
dependence structure the estimator is meant to handle, and confirm the
interval contains the true value at approximately the nominal rate.

**Generating process.** Two arms (A, B), $G/2$ clusters each, $m$ trials per
cluster. Cluster $g$'s success probability is drawn
$p_g \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ with
$\alpha = p(1-\rho)/\rho,\ \beta=(1-p)(1-\rho)/\rho$, the standard
beta-binomial cluster parametrisation, chosen because it has
$\mathrm{E}[p_g] = p$ **exactly**, for any $\rho \in (0,1)$ — so the true
marginal risk difference $\mathrm{RD} = p_A - p_B$ is known exactly by
construction, not approximately, and $\rho$ is the exact intraclass
correlation of the resulting binary outcomes within a cluster
($\mathrm{Var}(p_g) = \rho\, p(1-p)$). Trials within cluster $g$ are then i.i.d.
$\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_g)$. This is `analyze.selftest`'s
`_beta_binom_cluster` (check 29) and directly mirrors the study's own
structure: many correlated trials inside a cluster, clusters independent of
each other, an unpaired risk difference computed by pooling clusters within
each arm.

**Result, at the selftest's own configuration** ($p_A=0.55$, $p_B=0.25$, true
$\mathrm{RD}=0.30$, $\rho=0.15$, $G=24$ clusters ($12$/arm), $m=15$
trials/cluster, $B=400$, $N_{\text{sim}}=300$ synthetic datasets, coverage-sim
seed $= 20260804+999$, documented in `analyze.py`):

| interval | empirical coverage (nominal 95%) |
|---|---|
| percentile | **93.7%** (281/300 fixed via `analyze.py --selftest`) |
| BCa | **93.7%** |

Both track close to nominal — a percentile-point gap of 1.3pp is within Monte
Carlo noise at $N_{\text{sim}}=300$ (binomial SE $\approx\sqrt{.95\cdot.05/300}
\approx 1.26$pp). `analyze.py`'s check 29 asserts coverage $\ge 0.88$
(percentile) and $\ge 0.85$ (BCa) at this configuration — a wide margin below
the observed 93.7%, so the check has room to absorb run-to-run Monte Carlo
noise without becoming flaky, while still catching a broken implementation.
**Negative control, run once against this same generating process and NOT part
of `--selftest`** (it exists to justify the check above, not to be re-run every
time): a bootstrap that resamples individual TRIALS with replacement instead of
(model, attack_id) clusters — i.e. `analyze.cluster_bootstrap`'s exact mistake,
made deliberately, on data that still has $\rho=0.15$ intra-cluster
correlation — measures **75.3% coverage** (same $N_{\text{sim}}=300$, $B=400$,
seed) against the nominal 95%. The cluster-level implementation's 93.7% against
the trial-level mistake's 75.3%, on the identical synthetic data, is the
discriminating evidence that check 29 is testing the resampling UNIT and not
merely "does a bootstrap CI run".

**Coverage degrades — mildly, not catastrophically — as $G$ shrinks**, run
separately at the same $\rho=0.15$ and not asserted in the selftest (informational
only, so the suite does not become flaky at a configuration the study itself
does not need to run repeatedly):

| $G$ (total, both arms) | percentile coverage | BCa coverage |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 91.7% | 92.0% |
| 12 | 92.3% | 92.7% |
| 24 | 93.7% | 93.7% |
| 48 | 94.0% | 93.7% |

This is the honest analogue of §M6's small-$G$ sandwich table, on the bootstrap
instead: undercoverage is real at small $G$ (roughly 3–5pp below nominal at
$G=10$–12, in the same direction as the sandwich's bias) but far milder than
the sandwich's — §M6 measured **11.3% false-positive rate against a nominal
5%** (i.e. roughly 89% coverage, and that is with the *favourable* fully-crossed
design) at $G=5$ before the $t(G-1)$ correction, and even $t(G-1)$ only
restores nominal *size*, not interval *width* parity — intervals are 41.7%
wider as the price. The bootstrap's small-$G$ shortfall is smaller and it pays
no equivalent width tax to fix it, which is the case for preferring it over
the sandwich at this study's cluster counts — not a claim that either is exact
at $G=5$–6.

the CI-WIDTH gap between the resampling schemes is not constant across models
either. `gemma4:26b`'s **BCa** interval is the one exception that is
*narrower* than the exact interval (0.0049–0.1111 vs the exact 0.0000–0.1826),
which is exactly the case the divergence flag exists to surface:
`gemma4:26b` has $b=0$ pooled across all 680 pairs, so a large share of
bootstrap replicates also draw $b=0$ (or very close to it), piling the
bootstrap distribution up near the Haldane floor and producing the skew
($\hat z_0=-0.170$, $\hat a=-0.1584$) that the BCa correction is designed to
detect and that a plain percentile interval would report over-confidently —
`gemma4:26b`'s percentile/BCa width ratio (0.55) is the only one of the six
rows check-flagged as materially diverging. This is precisely the containment
finding's "universal but heterogeneous" character stated at full scale
($n=672$–$680$/model, `containment-heldout`) surviving a materially more
conservative resampling scheme: the ORDERING of the five per-model effects
(`gemma4:26b` strongest, `qwen3:30b-instruct` weakest) is unchanged, every
interval still excludes 1 (no model's containment effect is bootstrap-null),
but the WIDTH the exact conditional test reports is, on this evidence, an
understatement of the genuine attack-instance-level uncertainty — by roughly
5% for `gemma4:26b` up to more than 4$\times$ for `qwen3:30b-instruct`.

### M13.5 What this settles, and what it still does not

**Settles:** the pre-registration gap `PAPER.md` names explicitly (§7.5's
framing OR — "*neither interval is yet the pre-registered ... bootstrap*" —
and §7.6's containment OR, currently a probe-only section this appendix does
not otherwise touch) now has a computed interval, with a documented seed, that
resamples the pre-registered unit. Two different existing intervals are the
comparators here, not one: the framing OR has an existing model-clustered
GLM sandwich (Table 7) to compare against, and the bootstrap's percentile
lower bound agrees with it closely (3.37 vs 3.15); the containment ORs have
no usable sandwich at all — `containment_interaction`'s cluster-robust fit is
RANK-DEFICIENT at this run's $G=5$ (§M6's problem 2, `analyze.py`'s own
"SUPPRESSED — rank-deficient" guard fires on it) — so their only existing
comparator is the exact conditional McNemar interval, which assumes no
attack-level clustering at all. That the two independent methods (exact
McNemar; bootstrap) still order the five models identically, despite the
bootstrap's uniformly larger intervals, is corroboration of the *ranking*,
even though the sandwich comparison this study would prefer for the
*interval* is unavailable for containment specifically. Where bootstrap and
an existing interval disagree in width (`gemma4:26b`'s BCa interval;
the framing OR's upper bound, 30.78 vs the sandwich's 25.07), the bootstrap
is the interval to prefer: it needs no large-$G$ asymptotic and no assumption
that the sandwich's rank is sufficient for the parameter count, both of which
this study's $G=5$–6 genuinely strains.

**Does not settle:**

1. **$B=2000$ is finite.** The percentile/BCa quantiles carry Monte Carlo
   noise of their own, on top of the sampling noise the interval is meant to
   quantify. §M13.3's coverage simulation used $B=400$ per replicate and still
   achieved close-to-nominal coverage, which bounds this concern but does not
   eliminate it — a formal Monte Carlo error bound on the quantile estimates
   themselves is not computed here.
2. **The wild cluster bootstrap remains the documented open item** (References,
   below) for if $G$ ever grows enough that the ordinary pairs bootstrap starts
   to look small-sample-fragile itself; nothing here supersedes that entry.
3. **This validates the ESTIMATOR, not the STUDY DESIGN.** A cluster
   bootstrap correctly propagates whatever dependence is actually present in
   the (model, attack_id) grouping; it cannot manufacture information the
   design does not contain. At $G=5$–6 models, no resampling scheme turns that
   into a large-sample guarantee about the *model* dimension specifically —
   §M8's point about RQ2 applies here too, just one level removed: more
   attack instances sharpen these intervals (visible directly in the $G=170$
   vs $G=34$ containment rows above — same estimator, same models, and the
   coarser pooled cluster count is not simply five times the per-model one
   because the resampling variance does not add that way), more MODELS would
   be needed to say anything about the *model* population these five happen
   to sample from, and this bootstrap is silent on that question by
   construction.
